Free Printable Budget Tracker: A Monthly Worksheet That Fits on One Page

Most budgets fail for the same reason most diets fail: they're too complicated to keep up. A spreadsheet with forty categories and color-coded conditional formatting looks impressive in January and abandoned by March. This free printable budget tracker takes the opposite approach. It fits on a single page, tracks the handful of numbers that actually matter, and takes about ten minutes a month to fill in.

The idea is simple. Every dollar you earn either gets spent, saved, or quietly disappears. A one-page tracker makes the disappearing act impossible by forcing you to write down the plan before the month starts and the reality after it ends. Print a fresh copy each month, keep the old ones in a folder, and after three months you'll have something no budgeting app can give you: a clear picture of where your money really goes, in your own handwriting.

How to use the tracker

  1. Write the month at the top. Budgets are monthly because bills are monthly. One page, one month, no carryover confusion.
  2. List your income. Two lines are enough for most households: your main paycheck and any second source. Use the amount you actually take home, not the gross figure — the money in your account is the only money you can plan with.
  3. Fill in the budgeted column first. Before the month begins, write what you plan to spend in each of the eight categories: housing, groceries, utilities, transport, insurance, debt payoff, savings, and other. The "other" row catches everything that doesn't fit elsewhere, so small purchases don't vanish.
  4. Track the actual column as you go. Keep receipts in an envelope or jot totals on your phone, then update the sheet once or twice a week. Five minutes with a calculator is all it takes.
  5. Do the math at month's end. Subtract actual from budgeted in each row, then fill in the "money left" box: income minus everything you spent. That one number tells you whether the month worked.

Five habits that make a budget actually work

The worksheet is only half the system. These five habits do the heavy lifting:

1. Run a payday routine, not a monthly marathon. If you're paid weekly or biweekly, spend five minutes on payday moving money to where it belongs and updating the sheet. Small, frequent check-ins beat one exhausting end-of-month session you'll keep postponing.

2. Give problem categories a cash envelope. If groceries always blow past the plan, try withdrawing the budgeted amount in cash and leaving the cards at home. When the envelope is empty, the category is done. It's old-fashioned and it works, because handing over physical bills hurts in a way tapping a card doesn't.

3. Use the 48-hour rule for "other" spending. Most of the "other" row is impulse buys. When you want something unplanned, write it down and wait two days. If you still want it — and it fits the budget — buy it. Half the time you won't.

4. Review last month before planning the next. The real power of a paper tracker is the stack of old pages. Before filling in a new month, flip through the last two and circle the categories that kept going over. Adjust the plan instead of pretending you'll try harder — budgets should fit your life, not shame you.

5. Pay yourself with the "money left" box. If there's money left at month's end, move it to savings the same day instead of letting it sit. Money without a job gets spent. A budget that ends with "money left" is succeeding — protect the win.

What if the numbers don't add up?

Then the tracker just did its job. A budget that shows you're spending more than you earn isn't a failure — it's the first honest look at the problem, and you can't fix what you can't see. Start with the biggest gap: is it housing costs you can't change quickly, or variable spending you can? Cut one category by ten percent next month instead of overhauling everything at once. Progress compounds; perfection doesn't last.
